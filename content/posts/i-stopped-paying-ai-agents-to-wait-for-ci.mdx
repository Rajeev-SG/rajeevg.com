I found it in my own logs, and I have not stopped thinking about it since.

Four model responses in a row, a few days apart, whose entire job was to notice that nothing had changed. Each one pulled the whole working conversation back through the model — the instructions, the files, the edits, every previous turn — to produce a reply that amounted to: *still running, keep waiting.*

Together those four checks processed about **1.184 million tokens**. The new information they produced was worth roughly four words.

Here is the everyday version. I was paying a very expensive engineer to sit and refresh a delivery-tracking page. And every time she refreshed it, she first had to re-read the entire project history to remember why she was refreshing.

That was not a flaw in my setup. That was the setup. Or it was.

## What "checking again" actually costs

One sentence of background: CI — continuous integration — is the machinery that runs your tests and builds automatically whenever code changes.

When my coding agent finishes a change, the next question is whether CI agrees. The honest answer takes minutes. And here is the part I had never priced properly: for an AI agent, waiting is not quiet. The agent calls a wait tool, the wait ends, and then **the model has to be invoked again to decide what happens next**. A model call does not just look at the new status line. It pulls the entire conversation so far back through itself — everything it has read, written and decided — in order to produce five words.

So the cost of "let me check again" grows with the length of the session. Early in a task it is cheap. Six hours in, every status check is a full re-read of six hours of work, to learn that a progress bar has moved three percent.

I went and measured how big this had got.

## The audit: 612 responses that said nothing

From my AgentSessions records, every Codex session since **1 August 2026** — the same habit of auditing my own sessions that produced [the 551-session study](/blog/what-551-codex-sessions-reveal-about-real-ai-work), on a newer sample:

- **632 sessions**, containing **20,632 tool calls**
- **4,557 of those calls were waits**
- **612 model responses existed only to process a wait result** — nothing else in them
- those 612 responses processed roughly **77.75 million raw tokens**
- their total output was about **74,000 tokens** — around a thousandth of what went in
- almost all of it, about **77.54 million raw tokens**, ran on GPT-5.6 Sol: my strongest and least cheap model

Not every wait in that total is CI polling. Waits happen for builds, deploys, test runs, all of it. But the shape is the same everywhere: the model wakes up, re-reads its whole working life, and discovers that nothing has changed. The four-check sequence above is one clean recent example of the pattern — about 1.184 million raw tokens spent to answer one question.

One honest caveat, stated once, then we move on: **77.75 million raw processed tokens is not 77.75 million fully billed fresh tokens.** About 76.96 million of it was cached input, which providers charge for differently — usually far less. I am not going to convert that number into an invoice, because it is not one. The waste is more boring and just as real: 612 model turns that produced nothing, quota burned on re-reading, and a frontier model's attention spent on a progress bar.

## The fix is almost embarrassingly simple

A normal program can wait for free. A shell script can sleep for sixty seconds and cost nothing. A CI runner can poll a deployment for ten minutes and spend only CPU seconds. The expensive thing was never the waiting. It was *asking a model to notice* the waiting.

So I gave the agent a door:

1. **Do the useful work.** Write the change; run the checks that inform the next edit.
2. **Commit and hand off.** Emit a marker — `CI_HANDOFF` — that says: CI owns everything from here.
3. **Stop.** The authoring model's turn ends there. Not "check once more". Ends.
4. **CI waits, for free.** Tests, build, deployment wait, browser proof — all inside a runner that does not bill by the token.
5. **Failures wake someone cheap.** If something breaks, a small worker gets a compact packet: which commit, which job, the failing output. Not the full history of a successful run.

A green run ends in silence. Nothing wakes up to celebrate it, because nothing needs to: the green checks and the captured artifacts are the result.

## This article tested the idea on itself

The first version of this article was written by GPT-6 Astra at low reasoning. It wrote the piece in **2 minutes 56 seconds**, used **47,290 tokens**, committed, emitted `CI_HANDOFF`, and stopped existing. It never asked GitHub whether CI had finished. It never saw the deployment.

Everything after that handoff happened with no model in the loop: the local Article CI on my own machine ran the broad test suite, built the site, waited for the deployment, and drove a real browser over the published page, capturing about **2.96 MB of screenshots** as proof.

It was not flawless, and the flaw is the interesting part. The production CI timed out waiting for a deployment that had already succeeded: the workflow was looking for Vercel's green light in one corner of GitHub's reporting, while Vercel posts it in another. A human noticed in about a minute, the workflow learned to look in both places, it re-ran, and the proof passed. That is the failure path working as designed — one real failure, surfaced once, fixed once — instead of hundreds of model turns concluding "still running".

## What this proves, and what it does not

This is not a controlled experiment. I did not run matched tasks through the old and new paths and count the difference, and I will not claim a percentage saving from a sample of one. Anyone showing you such a number from a single run is selling something.

What it does show is architectural, and it is directly observable: the writing model stopped before several minutes of validation and deployment waiting, and that waiting happened somewhere that does not charge per token. Combined with the audit above, that is enough to change the default.

The metric I will track from here is deliberately tiny:

> **For a successful change, model tokens spent after `CI_HANDOFF` should be zero.**

Not "reduced". Zero. If CI is green, there is nothing left for a model to know. If CI is red, one cheap worker wakes with a compact packet, and that is the whole repair path. And if post-handoff turns start creeping back, or failures get harder to diagnose, then the handoff itself is what needs fixing — not the workers.

## The boring rule underneath

This is [why agent systems become slow, expensive and fragile](/blog/why-agent-systems-become-slow-expensive-and-fragile) in miniature: the useful work finishes, and then oversight keeps invoicing you for the aftermath. The cheapest model turn is the one that never happens — the argument I made in [Token-maxxing](/blog/token-maxxing-getting-more-useful-ai-work-for-less-money), applied to my own worst habit.

I stopped paying an AI to watch a progress bar. The progress bar is now watched by something that does not mind.
