01

The chain

In plain English When borrowing gets more expensive, buyers can pay less for the same house — and prices follow, slowly.

Six links run from the Bank of England's decision to the price of a home. The first three are arithmetic and happen within days. The last three depend on how people behave, which is why economists disagree.

Move Bank Rate

This assumes the market reads a rate move as lasting, so the five-year swap moves with Bank Rate. A move expected to be reversed barely shifts fixed rates at all — which is why your mortgage can ignore a hike entirely.

02

The monthly payment

In plain English A mortgage is a fixed monthly bill that slowly buys you the house. The rate decides how much of that bill is the house and how much is the bank.

Three things set the payment: how much you borrow, over how long, and at what rate. Only the rate is set by the economy — and it is the one that moves.

05

The words

In plain English Eleven terms do most of the work on this page. None of them is difficult once it is said out loud.

Bank Rate
The interest rate the Bank of England pays banks to park money with it. It is the lever for everything else, but it works slowly and indirectly.
Swap rate
What banks pay to borrow at a fixed rate for a set number of years. Your fixed mortgage is priced from this, not from Bank Rate — which is why fixed rates can move when Bank Rate does not.
Fixed rate
A mortgage whose interest rate is locked for a period, usually two or five years. After that you remortgage onto whatever rates are by then.
Loan-to-value (LTV)
The share of the property you borrow. A 75% LTV loan on a £200,000 flat is £150,000, with a £50,000 deposit.
Income multiple
How many times your salary a lender will let you borrow. Most UK lending is capped at 4.5 times, which is why incomes matter as much as interest rates.
Stamp duty (SDLT)
A tax on buying property in England and Northern Ireland, charged in slices and paid on completion. First-time buyers pay nothing below £300,000 — but lose the relief entirely at £500,001.
Nominal and real
Nominal is the number on the price tag. Real is that number after inflation — what the money actually buys. A price that rises 15% while prices generally rise 12% has really risen about 3%.
Valuation gap
How far house prices sit above or below what today's incomes and interest rates suggest they should be. The model expects the gap to close over roughly six years.
Buying power
The most a household can borrow, and therefore the most it can pay. It falls when rates rise, even if nothing about the house or the buyer changes.
Percentile (10th, 90th)
If you ran the next five years a hundred times, the 10th percentile is the result you would beat ninety times out of a hundred. It is a way of describing a range without pretending to know the answer.
Monte Carlo
Running the same model thousands of times, each with slightly different assumptions, to see how wide the range of plausible answers is.

Method and limits

In plain English Prices are anchored to what a household can pay each month. Everything else is a variation on that one idea.

The model

House prices are anchored to what a household can pay, and what a household can pay is a function of income and the interest rate:

A(r) = r / (1 − (1+r)−n)
annuity factor, 25 years
PTI*(r) = 6.00 × (A(5.88%) / A(r))1.10
equilibrium price-to-earnings at rate r
gt = yt − 0.12 · ln(PTIt−1/PTI*(rt−1)) + 0.132 · ln(At−1/At) + credit − supply

The two coefficients carry the argument. The 1.10 sets how much a change in rates moves the equilibrium: it works out at roughly 10.5% of value per percentage point of mortgage rate in the long run, at today's rate level. The 0.12 sets how fast prices get there — a half-life of about six years, because housing adjusts through transactions, not through a screen.

They were fitted to the four November 2026 forecasts simultaneously, not chosen. Root-mean-square error is 0.83 percentage points per year. The fit is worst for Savills, which is the most bearish on 2027 and which the model cannot explain from its own rate and earnings assumptions.

What it does not know

  • Regional detail. The model is national. Regional paths are the national path shifted by the five-year regional spread Savills publishes, which is a crude approximation of very different local markets.
  • Tax. Stamp duty is modelled at purchase because it is a real cash cost. Council tax, the £2m surcharge from 2028 and the 2027 rental-income changes are not.
  • Supply. Planning reform, and the 1.5 million homes target, are not in here as anything other than a dial you set yourself.
  • Nobody's forecast. The fan is not a prediction interval in the statistical sense. It is the spread you get when rate, earnings, credit and an unexplained residual all wobble at roughly the amplitude they have historically.
